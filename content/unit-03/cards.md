# Deck 1: Context: From the Mongols to Gunpowder

## Why the Mongols fell apart

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Power vacuum | A region left without a strong ruler, which rivals rush to take over; the breakup of the Mongol Empire left one across Asia. | Empty throne, many claimants. |
| Rival khanates | Family fights over who should rule split the Mongol Empire into four competing states. | One empire, four quarreling heirs. |
| Mongol assimilation | The khans adopted the religions and customs of the peoples they ruled, so they stopped acting as one people. | Rulers who became the ruled. |
| Ming expulsion of the Mongols (1368) | Chinese rebels drove the Yuan rulers out of China and founded a dynasty run by Han Chinese. | China for the Chinese again. |

## New powers rise

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Timur (Tamerlane) | Turkic-Mongol conqueror from Samarkand who raided Persia and India in the late 1300s and crushed the Ottomans in 1402; Babur descended from him. | Lame, ruthless, and an ancestor of the Mughals. |
| Ghazi ideal | A warrior model from the steppes that blended nomadic cooperation with fighting as a holy warrior for Islam. | Nomad plus holy warrior. |
| Gunpowder's origin | Invented in Song China and carried west along Mongol trade networks. | Song made it, Mongols moved it. |
| Shared roots of the Islamic empires | The founders of the Ottoman, Safavid and Mughal states descended from Turkic nomads of Central Asia and spoke a Turkic language. | Three empires, one steppe family tree. |
| Osman | Leader of a small Turkish state on the Byzantine border around 1300; the empire that grew from it was named for him. | The name behind Ottoman. |
| Armed trade | Empires exchanged goods with one another but kept troops and weapons ready in case another empire challenged their right to do business. | Trade with a sword nearby. |

## Gunpowder

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Gunpowder empires | Large states with many peoples that relied on cannons and firearms to conquer and control territory. | Cannons build empires. |

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Mehmed II | Ottoman sultan called the Conqueror (r. 1451–1481) who took the Byzantine capital in 1453 and made it his own capital. | The Conqueror. |

## Safavid Empire

| Term | Definition | Hook |

## Mughal Empire

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Babur | Descendant of Timur and Genghis Khan from Kabul who founded the Mughal Empire in 1526. | From Kabul to Delhi. |
| First Battle of Panipat (1526) | Babur's smaller army fought from behind linked carts with field cannons and matchlocks and beat the Sultan of Delhi. | Carts, cannons, a new empire. |
| Akbar | Mughal emperor (r. 1556–1605) who conquered most of northern India and won Hindu support with religious tolerance. | Tolerance as strategy. |
| Aurangzeb | Mughal emperor (r. 1658–1707) who pushed the empire to its largest size, brought back the jizya and enforced strict Sunni rule. | Biggest, then breaking. |
| Origin of the name Mughal | The dynasty's title comes from 'Mongol,' a sign of the founder's descent from Genghis Khan. | Mughal = Mongol. |

# Deck 3: Rivalries and Decline

## Wars between empires

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Battle of Chaldiran (1514) | Ottoman artillery and Janissaries beat Safavid cavalry, stopping Safavid expansion westward. | Guns beat horses. |
| Ottoman–Safavid rivalry | A conflict over territory, trade routes and the Sunni versus Shi'a split. | Land, silk and faith. |
| Trade embargo | An official ban on commerce; the Ottomans used them against Safavid silk merchants. | Starve the rival's market. |

| Mughal–Maratha conflict | Hindu fighters of the Deccan, led by Shivaji, used guerrilla tactics against Aurangzeb's armies. | Hit and run in the Deccan. |

## Decline

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Battle of Lepanto (1571) | A mostly Spanish and Venetian fleet defeated the Ottoman navy. | Ottoman sea power checked. |
| Second siege of Vienna (1683) | A failed Ottoman attack that marked a turning point in their power in Eastern Europe. | The tide turns. |

| Sick Man of Europe | A later nickname for the declining Ottoman Empire. | Still alive, but weak. |

# Deck 5: Belief Systems (3.3)

## Protestant Reformation

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Indulgences | Payments to the Church that were said to reduce punishment for sins. | Paying off sin. |
| Simony | Selling church offices. | Offices for sale. |
| Ninety-Five Theses (1517) | Martin Luther's list of charges against Church practices, which started the Reformation. | Nailed to a church door. |
| Martin Luther | German monk in Wittenberg who challenged the Church and was excommunicated in 1521. | The monk who said no. |
| Sola fide | Luther's teaching that salvation comes through faith alone, not good works or payments. | Faith alone. |
| John Calvin | French theologian who broke with the Church around 1530 and reformed the religious community of Geneva. | Geneva's reformer. |
| Predestination | Calvin's teaching that God has already chosen who will be saved (the elect). | Chosen in advance. |
| Huguenots | French Calvinists. | France's Protestants. |
| Protestant work ethic | The idea, from the historian Max Weber, that Calvinists labored hard and reinvested profits as a sign of God's favor. | Hard work as holiness. |
| Anglican Church | England's national faith, set up by Henry VIII with Parliament's approval and independent of the Pope. | The king's church. |

| Jesuits | The Society of Jesus, a Catholic order founded by Ignatius of Loyola and approved in 1540, which spread Catholicism through missions and schools. | Missionaries and teachers. |
| Francis Xavier | Jesuit missionary who reached Goa, India, in 1542 and Japan in 1549. | To Goa and Japan. |
| Inquisition | Church courts used to find and punish nonbelievers, sometimes with torture. | Hunting heresy. |
| Index of Prohibited Books | The Catholic list of banned writings, including Protestant Bibles and Copernicus. | The banned list. |

## Wars and settlements

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Peace of Augsburg (1555) | Let each German ruler choose whether his state would be Catholic or Lutheran. | The ruler picks the faith. |
| Henry IV of France | Protestant-raised king who became Catholic in 1593, reportedly saying 'Paris is well worth a Mass.' | Faith for peace. |
| Edict of Nantes (1598) | Henry IV's order allowing the Huguenots to practice their faith in France. | Toleration in France. |
| Revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) | Louis XIV ended toleration for the Huguenots, and many skilled craftsmen left France. | Toleration ends, talent leaves. |

| Spanish Armada (1588) | Philip II's fleet sent to conquer and convert England, defeated by English naval power. | Catholic crusade at sea. |
| Holy Synod | Council of clergymen overseen by a state official that Peter the Great created to run the Orthodox Church after abolishing the patriarch. | Church under the tsar. |

# Deck 6: Comparing Empires (3.4)

## Ruling diverse peoples

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Millet system | Ottoman arrangement letting each religious community run its own affairs. | Self-rule by faith. |
| Queue | The Manchu hairstyle that Han Chinese men were required to wear after 1644. | Haircut of loyalty. |
| Jizya | A tax that Muslim rulers charged non-Muslim subjects. | Tax on difference. |
| Akbar's Hindu policy | Akbar gave members of the majority faith government posts, married wives from it and exempted them from taxes on non-Muslims. | Partners, not subjects. |
| Aurangzeb's religious policy | He brought back the jizya, enforced strict Sunni rule and opposed Hindu influences, which fueled rebellions. | Strictness breeds revolt. |
| Qing legitimacy | As outsiders ruling a Han majority, the Manchus adopted Confucian traditions and the Mandate of Heaven while keeping some Manchu customs. | Look Chinese, stay Manchu. |

## Comparing systems

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Slave soldiers | Both the Ottoman sultan and the Safavid shah used enslaved troops to offset fighters loyal to tribes or local leaders. | Loyal because dependent. |
| Aztec tributary empire | The Mexica ruled through force, fear and payments from conquered peoples rather than a centralized bureaucracy. | Pay or be punished. |
| Inca provinces | The Sapa's realm in the Andes was divided into regions headed by nobles loyal to him, organized on a decimal system. | Counted in tens. |
| Songhai scholar-officials | The West African empire's rulers employed bureaucrats educated in the madrasas of Timbuktu. | Timbuktu trains the state. |
| Safavid–Mughal war over trade | Your textbook says religion mattered less in this rivalry than control of resources and commercial routes in present-day Afghanistan. | Follow the routes. |
| Gunpowder empires' decline | They fell behind European trading companies and military technology, suffered weak leaders and succession fights, and overtaxed their peasants. | Many causes, one result. |
