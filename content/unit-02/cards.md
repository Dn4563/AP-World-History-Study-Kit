# Deck 1: Silk Roads and Commercial Growth

## Why trade grew

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Intensification | After 1200, existing routes carried more trade over greater distances; the routes themselves were centuries old (revived by Arab merchants in the 8th and 9th centuries). | Same roads, bigger flow. |
| Mongol stability | The textbook's most significant cause of Silk Road growth: the Mongols united the routes under one authority that respected merchants, enforced laws, improved roads, and punished bandits. | Safe roads, more trade. |
| Luxury goods demand | Elites in Europe, Africa, and Asia wanted silk, porcelain, spices, and fine textiles, so artisans expanded production for export. | Status symbols drive supply. |
| Crusades (effect on demand) | Crusaders returned to Europe with eastern fabrics and spices, raising European demand for Asian luxury goods. | Knights came home wanting silk. |
| Caravan | A group of merchants and animals traveling together with guides; safer than traveling alone. | Safety in numbers. |
| Camel saddle (Silk Roads) | Travelers learned to design saddles that greatly increased the weight camels could carry. | Better saddle, bigger load. |

## Commercial innovations

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Caravanserai | A roadside inn (Persian for caravan plus palace) with stalls for animals and rooms and storage for travelers; made travel safer and cheaper. | Caravan plus palace. |
| Money economy | An economy that uses money instead of barter with goods like cowrie shells or salt; China had long had one. | Money, not barter. |
| Flying cash | Chinese credit system: deposit paper money in one place and withdraw the same amount elsewhere, because copper coins were too heavy to carry for big trades. | Cash that flies to another city. |
| Paper money | Currency in paper form, first used in China, which eased large transactions. | Light enough to carry. |
| Bill of exchange | A document promising payment of a set amount on a set date, letting merchants move money without carrying coins. | An IOU that travels. |
| Banking house | A precursor to modern banks where bills and credit were handled; European banking houses appeared in the 1300s. | Where the IOU gets cashed. |
| Improved, not invented | Caravanserai, credit, and paper money all existed before 1200; the key point is that they were improved and spread. | Upgrades, not inventions. |

## Cities and production

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Kashgar | Oasis city at the western edge of China where northern and southern Silk Road routes met; river water fed crops; once Buddhist, it became a center of Islamic scholarship; ravaged by Tamerlane in 1389–90. | Kashgar is the crossroads of China. |
| Samarkand | Trading city in present-day Uzbekistan between China and the Mediterranean; religiously diverse; known for artisans, mosques, and Islamic learning. | Stalls and scholars. |
| Proto-industrialization | Rising demand pushed China to expand iron, steel, and porcelain production for trade. | China's forges heat up. |
| Luxury production centers | Chinese silk and porcelain and Persian and Indian textiles all expanded for export. | China, Persia, India supply. |

# Deck 2: The Mongol Empire

## Rise and methods

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Temujin / Genghis Khan | Mongol leader (born 1162) elected "ruler of all" at the 1206 kuriltai; united the clans and promoted talent over family. Also spelled Chinggis. | Temujin becomes "ruler of all." |
| Kuriltai | A meeting of Mongol chieftains; the one in 1206 elected Temujin khan. | Council of chiefs. |
| Khan / khanate | A khan is a king; a khanate is his kingdom. | Khan king, khanate kingdom. |
| Mongol warfare | Skilled horse archers, strict discipline, feigned retreats, terror, and terrain mapping; the Mongols also adopted conquered peoples' technology. | Fast, fierce, borrowed tech. |
| Siege weapons | Portable towers and catapults used against walled fortifications, built with the help of captured Chinese and Persian engineers. | Walls fall to borrowed machines. |
| Mongol motive | The Mongols coveted the wealth of neighbors nearer the Silk Roads (silk, gold) and recruited skilled captives such as craftworkers, miners, and scribes. | Follow the luxury. |

## Four khanates

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Yuan Dynasty | Kublai Khan's Chinese dynasty (1271–1368); more Chinese in style, rebuilt the capital as Dadu, tolerated many religions; ended by the Ming. | Yuan is China. |
| Il-khanate | Khanate in Persia and Southwest Asia founded by Hulegu; Persians served as officials; the Mongols there later converted to Islam. | Il-khanate is Persia. |
| Golden Horde | Western khanate under Batu that conquered Russia and destroyed Kiev in 1240; ruled through Russian princes who paid tribute; lasted longest. | Golden Horde is Russia. |
| Chagatai Khanate | Central Asian khanate that stayed the most traditionally Mongol. | Chagatai is Central Asia. |
| Kublai Khan | Genghis Khan's grandson; Great Khan from 1260; founded the Yuan; welcomed foreigners such as Marco Polo. | Kublai, the Chinese-style khan. |
| Hulegu | Genghis Khan's grandson who destroyed Baghdad in 1258 and killed the caliph. | Hulegu equals Baghdad. |
| Indirect rule | The Golden Horde ruled northern Russia through existing Russian princes who sent regular tribute. | Rule through locals. |

## Pax Mongolica and transfers

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Pax Mongolica | "Mongol Peace" (c. 1250–1350): safer routes, maintained roads, punished banditry, and religious tolerance boosted trade. | Peace pays. |
| Yam | Mongol relay messenger system that linked the far reaches of the empire. | Mongol pony express. |
| Religious tolerance | Genghis Khan and Kublai Khan allowed many religions, unusual in the 13th century and helpful to trade. | Tolerate and trade. |
| Greco-Islamic medical knowledge | Greek medicine, advanced by Islamic scholars such as Ibn Sina, reached Western Europe through Mongol-era contact. | Greek to Islamic to Europe. |
| Numbering systems | Hindu-Arabic numerals, which began in India, reached Europe. | "Arabic" numbers began in India. |
| Uyghur script | Writing system adapted to represent the Mongol language and used to help run the empire. | Uyghur letters, Mongol words. |

## Decline

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Causes of Mongol decline | Succession disputes, vast distances, assimilation into local cultures, the Black Death, failed expansion (Japan, Java, others), and revolts. | Too big, too split. |
| Foreign officials (Yuan grievance) | Yuan rulers hired foreigners, dismantled the civil service exams, and stayed apart from the Chinese, angering the scholar-gentry. | Locals shut out. |
| White Lotus Society | Secret society that organized against Yuan rule in the 1350s. | White Lotus, rebel network. |
| Zhu Yuanzhang | Buddhist monk from a poor peasant family who led the revolt that overthrew the Yuan in 1368 and founded the Ming. | Peasant monk topples khan. |
| Tamerlane (Timur) | Conqueror who took over Central Asian territories around 1369 and ravaged Kashgar in 1389–90. | Timur takes Central Asia. |
| Mongol legacy | Largest continuous land empire in history; both destructive and connecting; spread ideas of centralized government. | Destroyers AND connectors. |

# Deck 3: The Indian Ocean

## Causes of growth

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Monsoon winds | Seasonal winds that reverse direction: winter winds from the northeast, spring and summer winds from the southwest; merchants timed voyages and waited in port for months. | Wait for the wind. |
| Lateen sail | Triangular sail that catches wind from many directions; used by Arab sailors (its origin is debated). | Triangle turns with the wind. |
| Stern rudder | Chinese invention at the back of the ship that improved stability and steering. | Rudder at the rear. |
| Magnetic compass | Chinese navigation tool that spread across the seas through trade. | Compass from China. |
| Astrolabe | Tool improved by Muslim navigators in the 12th century that shows how far north or south of the equator a ship is. | Astro means stars. |
| Dhow | Small wooden ship with lateen sails used by Arab and Indian sailors; dominated the seas of this period. | Dhow: Arab and Indian. |
| Junk | Large Chinese ship with multiple sails and a compartmented hull, up to 400 feet long. | Junk: the Chinese giant. |

## Goods and states

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Specialized products | Cotton, pepper, and steel from India; spices from the Spice Islands; gold, ivory, and slaves from the Swahili coast; silk and porcelain from China; horses and dates from Southwest Asia. | Every region had a specialty. |
| Calicut | Southwest Indian port known as the "City of Spices"; Arab and Chinese merchants met here and local rulers welcomed them. | Calicut, spice central. |
| Gujarat | Kingdom in western India that served as the go-between for East-West trade; customs revenue was huge. | Gujarat, the go-between. |
| Swahili city-states | East African coastal trading cities (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu) that exchanged gold and ivory for Chinese porcelain and Indian cotton; "Swahili" means "coasters," and the language blends Bantu and Arabic. | Coastal traders. |
| Sultanate of Malacca | Muslim city-state on the Strait of Malacca; grew rich from fees on passing ships and built a navy; fell to Portugal in 1511. | Toll booth with a navy. |
| Customs duties | Taxes on imported goods and port fees that gave states revenue from trade. | Tolls fund thrones. |

## Effects

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Diaspora | A settlement of people away from their homeland; settlers introduce their traditions into local cultures, which influence the settlers in turn. | Settle, share, swap. |
| Merchant intermarriage | Arab and Persian merchants stayed in Indian and East African ports and married local women, spreading Islam through family ties rather than missionaries or conquest. | Marriage, not missionaries. |
| Zheng He | Ming admiral who led seven voyages (1405–1433) with fleets of up to 300 ships to show Ming power, collect tribute, and open markets; reached Southeast Asia, India, Arabia, and East Africa. | Treasure fleet, tribute. |
| End of the voyages | Confucian scholars feared foreign contact and the cost; Yongle's successor ended the voyages and discouraged sailing abroad. | Cost plus Confucians equals stop. |
| Indian Ocean slave trade | East Africans were enslaved and sold to North Africa, the Middle East, and India, often for urban labor, household work, or soldiering. | Different from the Atlantic. |

# Deck 4: Trans-Saharan Trade

## Desert logistics

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Sahara and oases | Huge arid desert (about the size of China) where only small oases have water; caravans traveled oasis to oasis. | Oasis, the water stop. |
| Camel | Native to Arabia; drinks over 50 gallons quickly and travels long without water; replaced horses and donkeys in North Africa after 300 CE. | Built for the desert. |
| Camel saddle | Saddle developed by the Somalis to carry loads of up to 600 pounds; made heavy camel freight practical. | Saddle turns camel into cargo carrier. |
| Trans-Saharan caravan | Large organized groups with guides, provisions, and thousands of camels; family partners, credit, and agents linked cities. | Safety, supplies, trust. |

## Goods

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | The most precious trans-Saharan good, from West Africa; it drew foreign traders. | Gold goes north. |
| Salt | Came from North Africa (for example the Taghaza oasis) and was traded south for gold. | Salt goes south. |
| Trans-Saharan goods | South to north: gold, ivory, slaves, kola nuts. North to south: salt, textiles, horses, copper and brass. | Gold up, salt down. |
| Cowrie shells | Shells used as currency in West African trading cities. | Shells as cash. |

## Mali and Islam

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Mali Empire | West African empire that rose in the early 1200s; taxed and protected trade and became richer than Ghana. | Mali, the gold taxman. |
| Sundiata | Founder of Mali, the "Lion Prince," who returned from exile in 1235 and defeated his rivals; likely Muslim; built the gold trade. | Lion Prince, 1235. |
| Mansa Musa | Mali ruler whose lavish 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca displayed Mali's wealth; afterward built mosques and schools in Timbuktu. | Gold to Mecca. |
| Timbuktu | Mali city that became a world-renowned center of Islamic learning. | Timbuktu: books and gold. |
| Songhai | Kingdom that replaced Mali as the West African powerhouse in the late 1400s. | Songhai follows Mali. |
| Spread of Islam in West Africa | Spread by merchants and rulers' conversions rather than conquest; most West Africans kept traditional beliefs. | Merchants carried Islam. |

# Deck 5: Culture, Cities, and Travelers

## Religion and language

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Buddhism in East Asia | Came from India along the Silk Roads; the monk Xuanzang helped popularize it in China; Korea and Japan adopted it too. | India to China to Korea and Japan. |
| Chan (Zen) Buddhism | Syncretic faith blending Buddhism with Daoist ideas; popular with ordinary Chinese. | Buddhism plus Daoism. |
| Neo-Confucianism | Syncretic faith blending rational Confucian thought with Daoist and Buddhist ideas; Korea's state ideology; spread to Japan and Vietnam. | Confucius gets a remix. |
| Syncretism | The blending of beliefs from different religions into a new faith. | Blend, don't replace. |
| Hinduism and Buddhism in Southeast Asia | Spread by trade; the Khmer Empire's Angkor monuments show both Hindu and Buddhist influences. | Angkor shows both. |
| Islam in Africa and Asia | Spread by merchants, missionaries, and conquest; shaped Swahili culture, Timbuktu's learning, and South Asian architecture and language. | Islam rides the trade routes. |
| Urdu | Language blending Hindi with Arabic and Persian influences after Islam reached South Asia. | Hindi plus Persian plus Arabic. |

## Technology and learning

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Gunpowder | Chinese invention (used for weapons) that spread west and changed warfare. | China's boom. |
| Papermaking | Chinese technique that reached Europe in the 13th century; made books cheaper and raised literacy and record-keeping. | Paper makes books cheap. |
| Islamic scholars as translators | Translated Greek classics into Arabic, saving works by Aristotle and others, and added mathematics from India and advances in medicine. | Translators saved the Greeks. |

## Cities

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Hangzhou | Chinese city of about a million at the southern end of the Grand Canal; a center of culture and trade with a thriving Arab community; described by Marco Polo. | Hangzhou, Polo's wow. |
| Why cities grew | Political stability, safe transport, commerce, plentiful labor, and more farm output. | Safe, busy, fed. |
| Why cities declined | Political instability and invasion, disease, and falling farm productivity. | Fights, fever, famine. |
| Constantinople | Walled city weakened by the Fourth Crusade (1204) and plague (1346 and 1349), then fell to the Ottomans in 1453. | Constantinople crumbles. |

## Travelers

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Marco Polo | Venetian merchant who visited Kublai Khan's court and wrote about China (back in Italy by 1295); focused on trade and cities; many Europeans doubted him. | A merchant's eye. |
| Ibn Battuta | Moroccan Muslim scholar who began traveling at 21 (1325) and spent about 30 years visiting mostly Muslim lands, including Mali and China; Muslim point of view. | A scholar's eye. |
| Margery Kempe | English Christian mystic (c. 1373–c. 1440) who dictated a book about pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome, and Spain; a middle-class woman's life. | A pilgrim's eye. |
| Reading travel accounts | Travel accounts are primary sources shaped by the writer's religion, culture, and purpose, so always note the point of view. | Who wrote it and why? |

# Deck 6: Environmental Effects

## Crops

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Champa rice | Quick-ripening, drought- and flood-resistant rice that yielded two crops a year; came from Champa (central Vietnam), was offered to China as tribute, and fed population growth. | Fast rice, more people. |
| Bananas | Indonesian seafarers brought bananas to Sub-Saharan Africa; the nutritious food boosted population and let Bantu-speaking peoples settle where yams did not grow. | Bananas, Bantu boom. |
| Citrus crops | Spread through Muslim lands and markets (such as Samarkand) into the Mediterranean. | Citrus follows Islam. |
| Sugar and cotton | Caliphs' conquests spread their cultivation; later European demand for sugar fueled mass use of enslaved labor in the Americas. | Sweet, with a dark future. |

## Degradation

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental degradation | Larger populations strained the land: overgrazing, deforestation, and soil erosion cut farm output. | More people, thinner soil. |
| Overgrazing and soil erosion | Overgrazing near Great Zimbabwe forced people to abandon the city in the late 1400s; deforestation and soil erosion hurt feudal Europe. | Land wears out. |

## Plague

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Bubonic plague (Black Death) | Disease that spread along trade routes from Central Asia, reaching Europe in 1347 and peaking 1347–1351; killed about a third of Europe and many millions in Asia. | Trade carried it. |
| How the plague spread | Fleas on rats and other animals traveled on ships and caravans; Mongol conquests helped transmit it. | Fleas ride the caravans. |
| Effects of the plague | Labor shortages raised wages and weakened feudalism; trade and cities suffered. | Fewer workers, higher wages. |
| Boccaccio | Italian writer who lived through the plague; his Decameron was inspired by it. | Boccaccio witnessed it. |

# Deck 7: Comparing the Networks

## Similarities

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Shared origins | All the networks built on routes older than 1200 and grew as states and empires expanded. | Old routes, new traffic. |
| Stable states | Kingdoms, caliphates, city-states, and empires protected merchants and routes and funded upgrades such as compasses, lateen sails, saddles, and high-yield crops. | Safe trade needs strong rulers. |
| Trading cities as "knots" | Each network produced trading cities that held it together: Samarkand (Silk Roads), Calicut and Malacca (Indian Ocean), Timbuktu and Gao (trans-Saharan). | Cities tie the net. |
| Centralization | Trade wealth encouraged centralized planning, such as Malacca's navy and standardized currencies. | Wealth builds government. |
| Beyond goods | Merchants, diplomats, and missionaries exchanged ways of life as well as products. | Goods plus ideas. |

## Differences

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Silk Roads profile | Overland; luxury goods (silk, tea, porcelain, spices); horses, camels, and caravanserai; spread Buddhism, Neo-Confucianism, and Islam. | Land and luxuries. |
| Indian Ocean profile | Maritime; monsoon-dependent; goods too heavy to move by land; dhows and junks; compass, lateen sail, astrolabe, rudder. | Sea, bulk, monsoon. |
| Trans-Saharan profile | Desert; camel caravans; gold traded for salt; load-bearing saddles; spread Islam into Sub-Saharan Africa. | Desert, gold and salt. |
| Currencies | Silk and tin ingots, cowrie shells, then metal coins and Chinese flying cash. | Each region, its own money. |
| Maritime versus overland | As production rose, maritime trade volume began to exceed overland routes, requiring larger ships and better navigation. | Ships carry more. |

## Society

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Labor | Free peasants, artisans, debt labor, and enslaved people; trade in enslaved people was common along Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan routes. | Old labor, bigger demand. |
| Gender | Societies remained patriarchal; exceptions included Mongol women, Southeast Asian marketplace women, and European guild women. | Mostly patriarchal, some exceptions. |
| Centers of learning | Trade cities such as Canton, Samarkand, Timbuktu, Cairo, and Venice became educational centers. | Trade cities study too. |
| Environmental processes | Plague outbreaks and environmental strain (deforestation, overgrazing) accompanied growth. | Growth has costs. |
